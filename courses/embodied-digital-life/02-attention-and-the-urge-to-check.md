# Unit 2 — Attention and the urge to check

[Previous: Notice your digital pattern](01-notice-your-digital-pattern.md) · [Course](README.md) · [Next: Emotional momentum and breath](03-emotional-momentum-and-breath.md)

**Time:** about 25 minutes. **You need:** the fictional case below; no live feed, phone settings, or AI account is required.

**Learning outcome:** explain the difference between immediate interruption, social influence, and an unsupported claim about permanent brain change; design a choice that protects useful access.

## 1. Meet the pattern — 3 minutes

**Fictional case:** Dev is comparing two venue descriptions for a local event. A device signals that someone liked their latest post. Dev leaves the signal unanswered but keeps wondering who responded. Later they open the post, follow a recommendation, and forget which venue detail they were checking. The same device also provides a screen reader and a reminder they rely on.

What is competing for attention? Which functions would be lost if someone said, “Just switch off your device”? Describe the situation before deciding what Dev should do.

## 2. Separate two kinds of evidence — 6 minutes

**Immediate interruption.** In an experiment with university students, Stothart and colleagues found that phone notifications could disrupt performance on an attention-demanding task compared with no notifications, even without direct phone interaction. The text-message result was less conclusive because groups differed at baseline. This supports a possible immediate task cost, not a diagnosis or proof of lasting attention loss. [E1: notification study](../../research-and-reading/digital-life-evidence.md#e1-notifications-and-immediate-attention).

**Social feedback.** Sherman and colleagues studied adolescents aged 13–18 using a simulated social-media feed. Participants were more likely to endorse photographs shown with more likes, and popularity information was associated with differences in reward-related brain activity. Thirty-four adolescents participated; 32 contributed usable fMRI data. The study examined a specific laboratory situation. [E2: likes and adolescent responses](../../research-and-reading/digital-life-evidence.md#e2-social-feedback-and-adolescent-responses).

An fMRI signal reflects changes related to blood oxygenation; it is not a direct measurement of dopamine. Popular language about a “dopamine hit” often compresses several different ideas: noticing something new, anticipating a response, enjoying social approval, and repeating a behavior. These two studies do not establish a depleted chemical supply, a need for a dopamine reset, or permanent damage. They also do not establish that every adult responds like the adolescents in the second study.

You can take an urge to check seriously without turning it into a medical conclusion. For this lesson, the useful questions are concrete: What invited the check? What happened next? Was it what the person intended?

## 3. Make a choice map — 5 minutes

For Dev, complete the sequence below. Use description rather than blame.

1. **Invitation:** a like notification arrives.
2. **Possible response:** identify at least two choices, including one that preserves needed access.
3. **Immediate result:** what might happen to the venue task?
4. **Review point:** when could Dev decide whether the choice worked?

Then distinguish a personal setting from a shared expectation. Dev may choose to silence one nonessential notification category. If the event team expects immediate replies to every message, changing that expectation involves other people. If notifications cannot be adjusted separately, another format or arrangement may be needed.

This map is an original learning aid, not a model that diagnoses why an individual repeats a behavior. Use [Workbook section 2](workbook.md#2-interruption-plan) if useful.

## 4. Practice a pause in choosing — 3 minutes

Use the fictional case; you do not need to provoke an actual urge or open a feed. Read the phrase “a new response is available.” Then choose one of the following:

- Notice a comfortable external feature briefly, then return to the venue question.
- Remain with the text and write “now, later, or not needed” beside the imagined notification.
- Use [Practice A](practices.md#a-arrive-with-a-purpose) to restate the current task.
- Take an ordinary break or decline the practice.

If you notice an urge, you may describe it privately or leave it unnamed. There is no requirement to resist, become still, or obtain a calm state. This practice has not been tested as a treatment for compulsive use or as a way to restore attention.

## 5. Redesign the next ten minutes — 5 minutes

Write a plan that lets Dev compare the venues while retaining the screen reader and needed reminder. Include:

- One environmental or settings change that is possible without losing necessary access.
- One place to record or remember the comparison question.
- One chosen time or task boundary for reviewing non-urgent messages.
- A way to revise the plan if it makes participation harder.

If the task becomes an AI conversation, use the same purpose and review point. Before following a suggested next topic, ask whether it helps the current inquiry. A long or engaging conversation is not automatically a problem; compare it with the person's intention and the consequences they can observe.

## 6. Check your explanation — 3 minutes

**Claim A:** “A notification can interfere with the task even if I do not pick up the phone.” **Claim B:** “A notification damages dopamine, so everyone should remove all notifications.” Which is closer to the evidence?

**Response guidance:** A is supported for the conditions of the notification experiment. B adds a biological damage claim and a universal prescription the study did not establish. A good plan for Dev separates nonessential alerts from access functions and leaves room to discover that a different arrangement works better.

**Exit response:** give one practical boundary and one claim you would refuse to make from these studies. Your answer can disagree with a suggested setting if it explains the access need and offers another workable choice.

**Next use:** identify one invitation to change tasks and decide, deliberately, whether to follow it. No log, screen-time target, or public report is required.
