# Unit 6 — Return to choice

[Previous: Transitions into rest](05-transitions-into-rest.md) · [Course](README.md) · [Workbook](workbook.md)

**Time:** about 25 minutes for the complete lesson. An optional later experiment takes place within ordinary activities over a week; it is not required to complete the course.

**Learning outcome:** apply the course to a new situation, plan a reversible change, and explain what your observations can and cannot establish.

## 1. Name the capability you want to keep — 3 minutes

Choose one capability: returning to a task, retaining access during breaks, ending a session, distinguishing feeling from evidence, or explaining a claim's limits. Give an example of how it would matter in daily digital participation.

You may use a fictional person. You do not need to list symptoms, describe a relationship, reveal private messages, or show anyone your device history. The final task assesses an explanation and a plan.

## 2. Transfer to a new situation — 7 minutes

**Fictional case:** Jules is preparing an accessible handout for a community workshop. An AI proposes a confident but unsourced statement about social media “damaging dopamine.” Jules has worked longer than planned and is repeatedly accepting suggested revisions. A collaborator needs a draft, but has not asked for immediate publication. Jules uses a screen reader and an essential reminder. A teammate proposes turning every device off and leading everyone through counted breathing before approving the handout.

Answer these five questions in any accessible form:

1. What is the actual task, and what action has not been authorized?
2. Which claims or assumptions need checking? Name one relevant study or evidence distinction from the course.
3. What setting, schedule, or workflow change could help Jules make a deliberate decision while retaining access?
4. What optional practice or ordinary alternative could be offered? What must remain a choice?
5. What would count as a useful observation afterward? What would it fail to prove?

Aim for a short connected explanation. You can consult [the evidence ledger](../../research-and-reading/digital-life-evidence.md) or the lesson summaries. You do not need a live AI system or an academic-paper subscription.

## 3. Read a possible response — 4 minutes

**Response guidance:** “Jules is preparing a draft, and publication has not been authorized. The dopamine-damage claim needs a reliable source and a precise definition; the course's notification and adolescent social-feedback studies do not establish it. Jules can save the draft, mark the uncertain claim, and review it before further polishing. A bounded work period and a clear resuming point may be more practical than continuing through every suggestion. Needed reminders and the screen reader stay available. Someone can offer an ordinary break, external attention, a familiar small movement, or unaltered breath awareness, with a task-only alternative. Nobody should lead compulsory counted breathing. A clearer decision record or a more workable session would be observable. Calmness would not verify the handout, and a single session would not prove a health benefit.”

This is one defensible answer. Other choices can meet the goal if they preserve access, consent, evidence, and the distinction between drafting and publishing. For example, Jules could continue after checking the source if the deadline and their own assessment make that workable. A practice is not a prerequisite for sound judgment.

## 4. Plan one small experiment — 5 minutes

Choose one ordinary, low-stakes task you can reasonably repeat, or plan entirely for Jules. Use [Workbook section 6](workbook.md#6-a-small-reversible-experiment).

Record the following:

- **Question:** what practical change do you want to investigate? Example: “Does saving one resuming sentence make the next session easier to start?”
- **Baseline:** describe how the task usually works, or observe up to two ordinary sessions. Do not create extra strain to establish a baseline.
- **Change:** choose one adjustment for the next few sessions. If you combine a setting change with an optional practice, treat them as a package; your observations will not isolate which part mattered.
- **Observation:** choose a modest indicator, such as finding the next step, completing the intended comparison, or taking a break without missing information. A private description of usefulness is also possible. No health data is required.
- **Revision condition:** decide when to stop, adapt, or drop the change. New access barriers, unwanted burden, or discomfort are sufficient reasons.
- **Protected access:** name what must remain available, without explaining any private reason.

You may stop after writing the plan. If you try it over a week, the purpose is personal learning, not a clinical experiment or an effectiveness study. Do not involve another person, collect their behavior, or change their settings without their agreement.

## 5. Interpret a supplied record — 3 minutes

You can finish the learning task using this fictional record instead of collecting anything yourself:

| Session | Arrangement | Observation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | No resuming note; complex task | Needed time to find the next step |
| B | One resuming sentence; similar task | Located the next step readily |
| C | One resuming sentence; urgent interruption | Could not finish the planned work |
| D | One resuming sentence; easier task | Finished quickly |

What is promising? What remains uncertain? A careful interpretation is that the note may be useful for finding the next step. Task difficulty and interruptions differed, so speed and completion cannot be attributed solely to the note. There is no evidence here about brain repair, dopamine, sleep, or a medical condition. Keeping the note for its practical usefulness does not require making those claims.

## 6. Complete your final reflection — 3 minutes

Finish these sentences privately, aloud, or in thought:

- “One pattern I can now describe more precisely is ____.”
- “One change I would keep, test, or request is ____.”
- “One claim I would now qualify is ____ because ____.”
- “If my chosen practice is neutral, uncomfortable, or inaccessible, I can ____.”

**Completion check:** your response is ready when it identifies a concrete situation, gives a proportionate choice, protects access and voluntary participation, and states a limit on the conclusion. Revise any sentence that treats stillness, calm, bodily sensation, or compliance as proof of truth or success.

For another setting, try the [Commons transfer activity](https://github.com/TrivianInstitute/educator-commons/blob/main/assessment/commons-transfer.md). Continue into [Interaction Literacy](https://github.com/TrivianInstitute/interaction-literacy) when the next question concerns judgment, delegation, and responsibility in human–AI interaction.

The course is complete whether you performed every practice, selected alternatives, or used only fictional cases. You leave with a way to observe, decide, test modestly, and revise.
